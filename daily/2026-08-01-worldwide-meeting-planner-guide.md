# Worldwide Meeting Planner: How to Find a Fair Time Across Countries

Scheduling an international meeting looks simple until one person joins before sunrise and another stays online past midnight. A useful worldwide meeting planner should do more than convert one clock. It should help a team compare several cities, see working-hour overlap, and avoid daylight-saving mistakes.

## Why international meeting times go wrong

The most common mistake is treating time zones as fixed offsets. They are not. New York and London, for example, do not change clocks on the same dates. A recurring meeting that looks fair in February can quietly shift by one hour in March.

Other common problems include:

- comparing only the organizer's local time;
- forgetting half-hour and quarter-hour offsets;
- using ambiguous abbreviations;
- choosing a time that works today but fails after daylight-saving changes;
- sending a time without the date, city, or UTC reference.

## A practical method for planning a global meeting

### 1. Add cities, not abbreviations

Use exact locations such as Los Angeles, London, Mumbai, Singapore, and Sydney. City-based time zones are less ambiguous and automatically account for regional clock changes.

### 2. Compare the full day visually

A table or timeline makes trade-offs obvious. You can immediately see whether a proposed slot falls inside normal working hours, early morning, evening, or overnight for every participant.

### 3. Protect the least flexible participants

Start with people who cannot move their hours: customers, interview candidates, external partners, or teams with strict coverage windows. Then fit the more flexible internal participants around them.

### 4. Rotate recurring inconvenience

There may be no perfect time for teams spread across the Americas, Europe, and Asia-Pacific. Instead of forcing the same office to suffer every week, rotate between two or three acceptable slots.

### 5. Share one unambiguous result

Include the date, local times, cities, and a calendar invitation. For written announcements, add the UTC time as a stable reference.

## Example: New York, London, Singapore, and Sydney

A slot that lands in the New York morning may be comfortable for London but late for Singapore and Sydney. Moving it later helps the Americas and hurts Asia-Pacific. The right answer is often not a single permanent time, but a rotation that spreads the inconvenience fairly.

## Checklist before you schedule

- Confirm every participant's city or IANA time zone.
- Check the actual meeting date, not only today's offset.
- Mark each participant's acceptable working window.
- Avoid local holidays and regional clock-change weeks.
- Rotate difficult slots for recurring global meetings.
- Put the final local times in the calendar invitation.

## Final takeaway

International scheduling is not just time-zone conversion. It is a fairness problem. A good global meeting plan makes the inconvenience visible, accounts for daylight-saving changes, and gives the whole team one clear reference.
